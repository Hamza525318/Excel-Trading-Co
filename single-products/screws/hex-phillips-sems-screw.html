Installation Notes

  • ▸ The spring washer must be fully compressed flat for its anti-loosening effect to work — stop driving once it sits flush, not before.
  • ▸ Do not overtighten on thin sheet metal — the plain washer spreads load well, but excessive torque can still dish or crack a thin panel around the hole.
  • ▸ The washers spin freely on the shank — this is normal and does not indicate a fault. They are captive (cannot slide off), not fixed rigid.
  • ▸ Use a correctly sized Phillips bit in good condition — a worn bit on a hex-headed screw can cam out before the joint is properly seated, since there is no hex drive surface to fall back on.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a SEMS screw?

SEMS stands for "Sems" (originally a trademark, now used generically) — a screw pre-assembled at the factory with one or more washers held captive under the head. Instead of picking a screw and washer separately and fitting them by hand on the assembly line, the washer is already in place and free-spinning on the shank, so it can never be forgotten, dropped or fitted the wrong way round.

Why does this screw come with two different washers — a plain washer and a spring washer?

The two washers do different jobs. The DIN 125 plain washer sits against the work surface and spreads the clamping load over a wider area, protecting soft or painted surfaces from the screw head. The DIN 127B spring washer sits between the plain washer and the screw head — as the screw is tightened, it compresses flat and its spring tension resists the screw backing off under vibration.

Can the washers be removed, or are they permanently fixed?

The washers are captive, not permanently fixed. A small crimp or rolled step on the screw shank, below the washer stack, stops them sliding off the end — but they still spin freely on the shank and can be slid off over the threads if genuinely needed. "Captive" means they cannot be lost or fall off during handling and installation, not that they are welded or glued in place.

Should I choose steel zinc plated or stainless steel 304?

Steel with zinc/clear passivate plating is the economical choice for indoor, dry environments — the majority of switchboard, appliance and general fabrication assembly. Stainless steel 304 is worth the extra cost wherever the fastener is visible on a finished product, exposed to humidity, washdown or mild outdoor conditions, or used in food-adjacent or hygienic equipment where corrosion or rust staining is unacceptable.

What's the advantage over fitting a separate screw and washer by hand?

Functionally the clamped joint is similar, but a SEMS screw is a single part to pick, store and drive — no separate washer to keep in stock, no risk of an operator skipping the washer under time pressure, and no washer that can be fitted upside down or left out entirely. On a production line this measurably speeds up assembly and removes a common source of missed-part defects.
